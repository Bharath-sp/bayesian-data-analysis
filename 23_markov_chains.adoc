== Introduction ==
A Markov chain is a stochastic model describing a sequence of possible events in which the probability of each event depends only on the state attained in the previous event. They are used to represent a dynamic process (a process that changes with time). In particular, it concerns more about how the state of a process changes with time.

== Discrete State Markov Chain ==
Let's focus on discrete state space and discrete time Marko chain, i.e., a chain that can take discrete states and can jump from the current state to the next state after one unit time stem:[t].

Let's say, we want to model how the weather in a particular place changes over time (a day). We assume that the weather is constant throughout the day, and there can be only three possible states: Sunny, Cloudy, and Raining. For any process to be considered for modeling with Markov Chain, it has to assume the Markov Property.

Markov property states that, a state at time stem:[t+1] is dependent only on the current state at stem:[t] and is independent of all previous states from stem:[t-1, t-2, \dots]. In short, to know a future state, we just need to know the current state.

Thus, we can think of this simple weather model as a Markov chain in which there is a random variable with three possible values as stated above. These states are linked in a chain, with a directed arc from one day to the next.

image::.\images\markov_chain_01.png[align='center', 400, 300]

A Markov chain is a series of random variables stem:[z^{(0)}, z^{(1)}, \dots] that take discrete states, stem:[\{z_1, z_2, \dots, z_K\}] and there is a probability of transitioning from a state stem:[z_{i-1}] to another state stem:[z_i]: stem:[\mathbb{P}(z_i \, | \, z_{i-1})]. Formally, a discrete state Markov chain is specified with the following three items:

. Set of states stem:[\{z_1, \dots, z_K\}], where stem:[K] is the number of states.
. An initial probability distribution that describes the probabilities of occurrence of each state: stem:[p^{(0)}(z)] where stem:[z \in \{z_1, \dots, z_K\}]. That is, it gives a probability vector stem:[\{p^{(0)}(z_1), \dots, p^{(0)}(z_K)\}]. and these are marginal probabilities stem:[p^{(0)}(z_i)].
. Matrix stem:[\mathbf{T}] of transition probabilities (a matrix of probabilities of change in the state from one to another). This gives us the conditional probabilities, stem:[T(z_i,z_j) \equiv p^{(0)}(z_j \, | \, z_i)], transitioning from state stem:[z_j] to stem:[z_i].

The chain is homogeneous if the transition probabilities are the same for all stem:[i]: stem:[p(z_i \, | \, z_{i-1}) = T(i,i-1)]. That is, if the probability of going from state stem:[z_{i-1}] to state stem:[z_i] at time stem:[t] is stem:[T(z_i,z_{i-1})], then the probability of going from state stem:[z_{i-1}] to state stem:[z_i] at any other time stem:[t+n] is also stem:[T(z_i,z_{i-1})]. In short, the above graph remains the same (irrespective of when we observe it) for the entire observation period.

=== Example ===
Consider the following Markov chain with the transisition probability stem:[\mathbf{T}]:

image::.\images\markov_chain_02.png[align='center', 400, 300]

Here we have three states, stem:[k=3].

* Say we start with an initial probability distribution stem:[p^{(0)}(z) = (0.5, 0.2, 0.3)]. We sample a value from this distribution, stem:[z^{(0)}], it can be any of the three states stem:[z_1, z_2] or stem:[z_3], but more likely stem:[z_1]. This is stem:[m=0] step.

* Then, at the next step stem:[m=1], we have the probability distribution over the states given by stem:[p^{(1)}(z) = p^{(0)}(z) \cdot \mathbf{T} = (0.18, 0.64, 0.18)]. For example, the probability of stem:[z^{(1)}= z_2] (the second RV in the chain taking state stem:[z_2]) is computed as:
+
** Being in state stem:[z_1] in the previous step, and transitioning to stem:[z_2 = 0.5 * 1].
** Being in state stem:[z_2] in the previous step, and transitioning to stem:[z_2 = 0.2 * 0.1].
** Being in state stem:[z_3] in the previous step, and transitioning to stem:[z_2 = 0.3 * 0.4].
+
Thus, we get stem:[p^{(1)}(z_2) = p^{(0)}(z_2 \, | \, z_1) p^{(0)}(z_1) + p^{(0)}(z_2 \, | \, z_2) p^{(0)}(z_2) + p^{(0)}(z_2 \, | \, z_3) p^{(0)}(z_3) = 0.5 + 0.02 + 0.12 = 0.64]. Similarly, we can compute the probabilities of other states to get stem:[p^{(1)}(z)= (0.18, 0.64, 0.18)]. Then, we sample a value from this distribution, stem:[z^{(1)}].
* At stem:[m=2], stem:[p^{(2)}(z) = p^{(1)}(z) \cdot \mathbf{T} =  p^{(0)}(z) \cdot \mathbf{T}^2]

* At stem:[m = M] step, it will be stem:[p^{(M)}(z) = p^{(0)}(z) \cdot \mathbf{T}^M].

If the sequence transits for a large number of times, what will be the resulting stem:[p(z)], that is the probability (in the limit as stem:[M \to \infty]) for each state? In this case, it will be stem:[p^*(z) = (0.2, 0.4, 0.4)].

As we repeat this process, for certain transition matrices stem:[\mathbf{T}], the probability distribution over states converges to one single distribution stem:[p^*(z)]. After this point, we get stem:[p^*(z) = p^*(z) \cdot \mathbf{T}]. Such stem:[p^*(z)] is known as an *invariant* or *stationary* distribution of the chain.

CAUTION: The convergence happens only for certain transition matrices stem:[\mathbf{T}] for a given chain.

After this burn-in period, if we continue the process, we will be moving over the states according to stem:[\mathbf{T}] but the distribution over the states stem:[p^*(z)] remains constant. These states that we move over can be seen as samples from the invariant distribution stem:[p^*(z)].

== Convergence Criteria ==
A Markov chain stem:[(X_0, X_1, \dots)] will converge to a stationary distribution if it satisfies: Irreducibility, Aperiodicity, and Finiteness (the state space stem:[S] is finite).

. Irreducibility: For every pair of states stem:[z_i] and stem:[z_j] in stem:[S], there exists a finite number of steps stem:[m] such that, starting from state stem:[z_i], there is a positive probability of reaching state stem:[z_j] in stem:[m] steps. In other words, the Markov chain can reach any state from any other state *eventually* (in a finite number of steps), with non-zero probability. 
+
[stem]
++++
\forall z_i, z_j \in S, \exists m < \infty: \mathbb{P}(X_{n+m} = z_j \mid X_n = z_i) > 0
++++
+
This ensures reachability in some stem:[m] steps.

. Aperiodicity: The chain should not get trapped in cycles (a subset of states in which the chain remains once it transits to one of these states). It rules out the possibility that some transitions only occur at multiples of a specific period. There should be a positive probability to escape a cycle and reach some other state.

*Example:*

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
++++

This is:

* Irreducible: We can go from 0 to 1 and vice versa.
* Not aperiodic: We can only return to our starting state in even steps.

We get stem:[\mathbf{T}^n = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}] for any even stem:[n] and stem:[\mathbf{T}^n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}] for any odd stem:[n]. Note that not all the elements of the matrix stem:[\mathbf{T}^n(z_i, z_j) > 0] in the limiting case.

====
Theorem:

Let stem:[(X_0, X_1, \dots)] be an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain with finite state space stem:[S = \{z_1, \dots, z_K\}] and transition matrix stem:[\mathbf{T}]. Then there exists an integer stem:[M < \infty] such that for all stem:[n \geq M], all entries of the matrix stem:[\mathbf{T}^n] are strictly positive.

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{T}^n(z_i,z_j) > 0 \quad \text{for all } z_i,z_j \in S \text{ and all } n \geq M
++++
====

This condition is sufficient to imply that there is a *unique* invariant distribution stem:[p^*(z)] that satisfies stem:[p^*(z) \cdot \mathbf{T} = p^*(z)] and stem:[\sum_{k=1}^K  p^*(z_k) = 1]. The vector stem:[p^*(z)] is the left eigenvector of the matrix stem:[\mathbf{T}] with corresponding eigenvalue 1. For two states case stem:[K=2], this can be written as:

[stem, id='eq_1']
++++
\begin{align}
p^*(z) & = p^*(z) \cdot \mathbf{T} \nonumber \\
\begin{bmatrix} p^*(z_1) & p^*(z_2) \end{bmatrix} & = \begin{bmatrix} p^*(z_1) & p^*(z_2) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T_{11} & T_{12} \\ T_{21} & T_{22} \end{bmatrix} \nonumber \\
\nonumber \\
\begin{bmatrix} p^*(z_1) & p^*(z_2) \end{bmatrix} & = \begin{bmatrix} p^*(z_1) T_{11} + p^*(z_2) T_{21} & p^*(z_1) T_{12} + p^*(z_2) T_{22} \end{bmatrix} \nonumber \\
\nonumber \\
p^*(z_j) & = \sum_{i=1}^K p^*(z_i) \cdot T(z_i,z_j) \text{ for } j=1, \dots, K
\end{align}
++++

This is the distribution that the distribution of stem:[X_n] converges to as stem:[n \to \infty], regardless of the initial state.

[stem]
++++
\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{T}^n(z_i,z_j) = p^*(z_j) \quad \forall z_i, z_j \in S
++++

The Markov chain converges to the stationary distribution only after a certain period. The time taken by Markov chain to converge to the stationary distribution is known as *burn-in period*.

====
A sufficient (but not necessary) condition for ensuring that the required distribution stem:[p(z)] is invariant is to choose the transition probabilities to satisfy the property of detailed balance, defined by

[stem, id='eq_2']
++++
\begin{align}
p(z_i) \, T(z_i, z_j) = p(z_j) \, T(z_j, z_i)
\end{align}
++++
====

This condition helps us check if a distribution stem:[p(\mathbf{z})] is an invariant distribution corresponding to a transition stem:[T].

[stem]
++++
\begin{align*}
\sum_{i} p(z_i) \, T(z_i, z_j) & = \sum_{i} p(z_j) \, T(z_j, z_i) \\
& = p(z_j) \sum_{i} T(z_j, z_i) \\
& = p(z_j) \sum_{i} P(z_i \, | \, z_j) = p(z_j)
\end{align*}
++++

We prove <<eq_1, Equation 1>>. Therefore, if some stem:[p(z)] holds the detailed balance condition <<eq_2, Equation 2>>, then that stem:[p(z)] is an invariant distribution for the transition probability matrix stem:[T].

== Continuous State Markov Chain ==
Here let's focus on continuous state, discrete time Markov chain. Here the random variable can take continuous values rather than discrete states. And let's also deal with random vector instead of a single random variable.

Let's define the Markov chain to be a series of random variables stem:[\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(M)}] with the first-order Markov property:

[stem]
++++
p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \, | \, \mathbf{z}^{(m)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(1)}) = p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \, | \, \mathbf{z}^{(m)})
++++

for stem:[m \in \{1, \dots, M-1\}].

We specify the Markov chain by specifying the initial distribution stem:[p^{(0)}(\mathbf{z})] and the transition probability stem:[p^{(0)}(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \, | \, \mathbf{z}^{m}) \equiv T(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}, \mathbf{z}^{m+1})] (the conditional distribution over the next state given the current state).

Here stem:[\mathbf{T}] is not a matrix, it is referred to as *transition Kernel*. This is a transition probability density that gives the likelihood of proposing a move to stem:[\mathbf{z}^{(m)}], given the current state stem:[\mathbf{z}'].

The marginal probability for a particular variable can be expressed in terms of the marginal probability for the previous variable in the chain:

[stem]
++++
p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)}) = \int p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \, | \, \mathbf{z}^{(m)}) \, p(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}) d\mathbf{z}^{(m)}
++++

This is analogous to the discrete variable case where the integral was replaced by a summation. A distributions stem:[p(\mathbf{z})] is said to be invariant with respect to a Markov chain if each step in the chain leaves that distribution invariant. The distribution stem:[p(\mathbf{z})] is invariant if

[stem]
++++
p(\mathbf{z}) = \int p(\mathbf{z}') \, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) \, d\mathbf{z}'
++++

This is the equivalent version of <<eq_1, Equation 1>> for the continuous random variable case.

A sufficient (but not necessary) condition for ensuring that the required distribution stem:[p(\mathbf{z})] is the invariant distribution of the chain is to choose the transition probability (or transition Kernel) such that it satisfies the property of detailed balance, defined by

[stem]
++++
p(\mathbf{z}) \, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}') = p(\mathbf{z}') \, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z})
++++

where stem:[\mathbf{z}'] is the previous state and stem:[\mathbf{z}] is the current state. We fix the invariant distribution stem:[p(\mathbf{z})] first, and choose the appropriate transition kernel.

This is the equivalent version of <<eq_2, Equation 2>> for the continuous random variable case. It is easily seen that a transition kernel that satisfies detailed balance with respect to a particular distribution will leave that distribution invariant, because

[stem]
++++
\begin{align*}
p(\mathbf{z}') \, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) & = p(\mathbf{z}) \, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}')  \\
\int p(\mathbf{z}') \, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) \, d\mathbf{z}' & = \int p(\mathbf{z}) \, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}')  \, d\mathbf{z}' \\
& = p(\mathbf{z}) \int \, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}')  \, d\mathbf{z}' \\
& = p(\mathbf{z}) \int \, p(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z})  \, d\mathbf{z}' = p(\mathbf{z})
\end{align*}
++++
